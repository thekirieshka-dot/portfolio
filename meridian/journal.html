(00) JournalSix notes from the benches

Notes from the bench.

Written by whoever had something to say, and edited by nobody who has not held a loupe. Open any note to read it.

  1. A Calibre M1 takes four synthetic oils and one grease, put on with an oiler whose tip has been drawn down to 0.2 mm. A good drop is the size of a full stop on this page, and it is gone from the bench in a second. Too much and it creeps; too little and a jewel runs dry.

    That arithmetic is the reason for the service interval. Oil does not fail all at once. It thickens, and the rate starts to wander in the vertical positions first. It is the first thing we look at when a watch comes home.

    — Samuel Huguenin, bench 06

  2. Under the barrel bridge a chamfer runs round every edge at forty-five degrees, no wider than a hair. At 3.5× you can see it. At arm’s length you cannot, and it adds six hours to a movement.

    We do not do it for the owner, who will open the caseback twice in a decade. We do it because it is the only honest test of whether a hand and an eye were paying attention, and because the next watchmaker to open the case will know at once.

    — Noémie Jacot, bench 05

  3. Note 03On the sweep seconds handSix steps a second, and the part you will look at longest.7 min

  4. Six watchmakers, fifty movements each, and a timing room that holds twenty-one days of work at a time. The limit is not a marketing figure; it is the arithmetic of one room. We tried fifty-five a head in 2019 and the rate sheets got worse.

    Nobody has yet asked for more in a form we could say yes to. When someone does, we will show them the six benches and the one timing room.

    — Cédric Matthey, bench 02

  5. A healthy balance has a dry, even tick, six to the second, like rain on a tin roof a long way off. A movement with a burr on a pivot has a slight limp in it. Cédric can hear it from the next bench, over the stove.

    The timing machine will confirm it a minute later, to a tenth of a second a day. But the ear gets there first, and every watchmaker here trusts it for that reason.

    — Joël Perrenoud, bench 03

  6. We cut and wore thirty-one cases in plaster, resin and steel, between 34 mm and 42 mm. Everyone started out wanting forty. Thirty-eight was the one nobody could stop wearing and nobody could explain.

    Lug to lug it is 45.5 mm, which is the number that matters on a narrow wrist. The diameter was just the one we remembered.

    — Léa Robert, bench 04

Note 03Seven minutesAline Vuilleumier, bench 01

On the sweep seconds hand.

Six steps a second, and the part of the watch you will look at longest.

0 % read
aHand length 17.4 mm from the pivot. The tail behind it is 5.1 mm and carries the counterpoise.

Look at a Calibre M1 for long enough and you stop seeing the hours and the minutes and begin to see only the third hand. It is the one part of the watch that is always moving, which makes it the one part that is always being judged.

b21,600 vibrations an hour is six beats a second. A quartz watch steps once a second; this one steps six times.

It does not sweep, whatever the word suggests. The balance beats six times a second, and each beat lets the escape wheel advance by one tooth’s worth of travel. The hand moves in six small steps, one degree each. Your eye, which cannot count that fast, rounds them into a glide.

A sweep is six small decisions a second that your eye refuses to count.

Fig. 11Bench 01. The hand goes on last, and is fitted by the person who will sign the movement.

cThe lever that presses it home gives way at twenty grams. The pivot is barely thicker than a hair.

That rounding is the whole trick, and it is unforgiving. If the hand is fitted a hair off-centre you will not see it at twelve or at six. You will see it at nine, and again at three, a hundred times a day, for the rest of the watch’s life. So Aline fits every hand herself, with the dial held under a loupe and the pivot supported from beneath, and presses it home with a lever that gives way before the pivot does.

dChecked at every five-second mark: 0, 5, 10 … 55. Tolerance 0.01 mm.

The check afterwards is dull and absolute. With the movement on the timing bench, the hand is carried round a full minute under a 10× glass, and its tip is read against the minute track at every five. If it is more than a hundredth of a millimetre off the line at any of them, the hand comes off and goes on again.

There is a smaller, older reason to care. A hand that runs is a hand that tells you the watch is alive. Someone who winds a Meridian in the morning does not look first at the time. They look at the third hand, find it moving, and only then, a little reassured, ask what the time is.

A.V.Aline Vuilleumier
Bench 01, since 2011
