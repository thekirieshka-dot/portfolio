Short notes about things we have built and the habits behind them. We write them when a drawing teaches us something, not on a schedule.

(A)NotesNewest first, undated

On every project we draw the sun before the walls. This note follows one living room in Copenhagen through five hours of light and shows what a sun path does to a plan.

D-04, sun path and plan
By
Ida Thorsen

A machine can put forty square metres of plaster on a wall in an afternoon. A plasterer puts on eight, and by the end of the day the wall has a grain, a direction and a small history of the arm that made it.

Lime wants time. Each coat has to take up water slowly and give it back, so the work is paced by the weather and not by the programme. We write that into the schedule on the first day, and clients are surprised for about a week.

What you get is a surface that is never quite flat and never quite the same colour twice, which turns out to be the thing people say they love about the room.

Stair House
Where
Three coats, hand-floated

The Stair House is 168 square metres on a plot four metres wide. We gave the stair the best light and let the rooms take what was left.

The treads are cast in lime against the wall, so there is no joint between the stair and the plaster. The rail is the wall itself, rounded to the width of a hand where a hand will go. The roof window above grades the light from white to sand as it falls.

On the first morning the client’s daughter sat on the fourth step and did not move for an hour. We took it as a review.

The flat in Frederiksberg had eleven doors. The client wanted more space and we could not make any, so we took away four doors and hung linen in the openings.

A door says yes or no. A curtain says perhaps, and it moves with the draught and with the person. The rooms became larger without a single wall changing.

We kept two doors, solid oak and pivot-hung, for the places where a no is the point: the bathroom, and the bedroom at night.

Project
Curtain Apartment
Where
Frederiksberg, 2021
Read
2 min

The municipal hall stands on thirty-six steel columns so that the ground can stay a square. Each one is a hollow tube, 240 millimetres across, hot-blackened and waxed by hand.

We argued for a long time about whether they should be round. Square columns make corners; round ones make a field. A field you walk through, and on Saturdays the market sets up between the columns.

Cyclists lean on them. The wax takes the mark of a shoulder and loses it by evening. We have stopped thinking of that as maintenance.

(B)Note 01Ida Thorsen · 7 min

Drawing the daylight first.

05:30Before the walls

Every project we draw begins with a sheet that has no walls on it. There is a plot, a north arrow and an arc of sun. We call it the daylight drawing, and in the first month it is usually the only sheet the client understands completely.

The arc is the same for everyone in Copenhagen. At 55.7 degrees north the midsummer sun climbs to 58 degrees and the midwinter sun barely clears 11. What changes from plot to plot is only what is standing in front of it.

Sun positions are computed for 55.7° N, in solar time. The diagram follows the text: scroll, and the year and the hour move together.

07:30Morning

Morning is the kitchen. A kitchen window faces east, or a little south of it, and the sun arrives low enough to reach the far wall by the time the kettle boils. In the Curtain Apartment we moved the kitchen across the flat to take the east window, and gave the old kitchen to a bed.

A window is a decision about an hour.

Equinox, 07:30: altitude 12°, azimuth 109°, east-south-east.

East window 1 200 wide, sill at 900.

12:00Noon

At noon the sun is in the south and, in June, nearly overhead. The south window then does the opposite of what we want: it overheats the room. A deep reveal fixes that. The wall is 480 millimetres of brick, so in midsummer the head of the window throws a shadow down over half the glass, and in December the low sun still reaches the back of the room.

That is the whole argument for thick walls, drawn in one line.

A 480 mm reveal drops a 750 mm shadow from the head of the window at noon in June.

14:30The low sun

Between November and January the sun never climbs above fifteen degrees, and by three in the afternoon it is already setting. We draw the patch of light it throws on the floor at half past two on the shortest day and ask whether anyone would want to be sitting in it. If yes, the long table goes there.

Midwinter, 14:30: altitude 5°, azimuth 214°. Sunset is at about 15:20 solar time.

19:00Evening

In June the evening light is long and it comes from the north-west, through the window nobody was thinking about. The last thing we draw is the one a client mentions in the final meeting: where does the evening go? Often it goes to a small west window by the stair, no bigger than a book.

The drawing has no walls on it until all five hours have an answer.

Midsummer, 19:00: altitude 11°, azimuth 295°, north-west.

West window 400 wide, sill at 1 200.
