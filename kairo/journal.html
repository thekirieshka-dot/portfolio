(Journal) Notes from setFive, written by whoever was standing nearest

  1. We rehearse with the camera running. It sounds reckless and it is the cheapest rule we have: the actor stops rehearsing and starts living the scene somewhere around the third pass, and by then the operator has already learned where she is going to stand.

    On White Hours we walked the corridor eleven times. The model was told it was only a rehearsal for the light. On the ninth walk she stopped looking for the lens and looked at the end of the corridor instead, and that is the shot in the film. It was never a take. Nobody said action.

    The price is film. We shoot about forty per cent more negative than we would if we waited for ready. We think it is the only part of the budget that pays back every single time.

  2. A 400 ft roll of 16 mm runs for eleven minutes and a little over, and it costs roughly what a good dinner for the whole crew costs. Nobody on a digital set has ever thought about that sentence, and everyone on a film set has.

    Last year we exposed 41,000 feet: sixty-three rolls of black and white, twenty-eight of tungsten colour and eleven of the fine-grain daylight. We keep the count on a whiteboard by the stock fridge, in Hanne’s handwriting, updated at wrap. The number is not a budget line. It is a way of making sure that somebody says out loud, before a take, that this one costs something.

    The camera magazine holds ten minutes of silence between the director and the rest of the set. That is the real reason we shoot on film.

  3. Most music videos are shot to the song: a speaker, a click, performers miming to a track they have heard two hundred times. We stopped doing that in 2021. On Low Tide there was no speaker on the beach, only wind at forty kilometres an hour and two brothers who did not have to act as if they could hear anything.

    The song arrived in the edit and it arrived late on purpose. Yusuf laid it under the picture on the third day of the cut, with the sound of the sea still running under it, and the shots that worked were not the ones we had expected. People move differently when they are not keeping time.

    Noor still keeps a small speaker in the van, switched off. For the record, the band has asked twice for a version with playback. We have said no twice, quickly.

  4. Verre make architectural glass, and the brief was to film the light that comes through it. The arcade we found has a gap in its roof, and for twelve minutes each morning in March the sun clears the building opposite and lays stripes across the floor, sharp as a ruled page.

    Twelve minutes is not a lot of time. We rehearsed the move on the first morning with the camera empty. We shot on the second. On the third, the light came in nine degrees further south than the day before, and Ilka said, without looking up, that we should keep that one. The film is cut from the third morning.

    We build every schedule backwards from the light now. We ask for a location in the afternoon, we look at it at seven, and then we agree on the time of day.

  5. It is the oldest rule in the building and the one clients ask about first. On a shoot day we stop at 13:00, every time, and we sit down. A table, chairs, hot food, no plates on laps. The director sits next to the runner.

    We have never been able to prove that it makes better films. We can say that the afternoon is always better than the morning, that nobody has ever walked off a set that ate together, and that the most useful conversation of any day usually happens over the second helping, when nobody is holding a phone.

    The kitchen in Kreuzberg has a table with fourteen chairs. We have never had fewer than twelve people on a commercial, so on those days the others stand at the counter, and we count that as seated.
